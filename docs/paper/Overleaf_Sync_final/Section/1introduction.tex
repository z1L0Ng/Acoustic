\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\revisiondone\par

Listening to breathing sounds offers a simple, non-invasive way to assess
respiratory health. Identifying abnormal sounds such as crackles and wheezes
is an important part of this assessment, motivating automated methods that
can assist their recognition\cite{hsu2021hflung}. Developing these systems
depends on expert-labeled recordings, which are expensive to obtain at
scale\cite{zhang2024opera}. Existing respiratory sound datasets provide
annotated examples collected from different patient populations and
recording conditions\cite{rocha2019icbhi,zhang2022sprsound}. Using these
resources together offers an opportunity to make fuller use of the
supervision already available.

Prior work has improved respiratory sound recognition through data
augmentation and contrastive learning
\cite{gairola2021respirenet,bae2023patchmix}, training that accounts for
patient and device variation\cite{kim2024sgscl,jeong2025pafa}, and audio
pretraining
\cite{chen2023beats,zhang2024opera,niizumi2025m2dresp,niizumi2025foundationeval}.
For reusing annotated datasets, SPRSound explores data fusion with merged
categories\cite{zhang2022sprsound}, while LungMix studies transfer after
mapping datasets to a common classification target\cite{ge2025lungmix}.
Multi-breath and PC-MCL encode co-occurring sound categories through
multi-label supervision within the ICBHI task
\cite{chua2024multibreath,jeong2026pcmcl}. More general sound-event learning
supports joint training with heterogeneous and missing
labels\cite{cornell2024dcase}. However, the respiratory formulations above
leave open how to jointly use annotations that differ in granularity and
task definition. ICBHI assigns sound-type labels to breathing cycles,
whereas SPRSound provides detailed event annotations but evaluates
normal/abnormal recognition in the task studied here
\cite{rocha2019icbhi,zhang2022sprsound}. These annotations describe related
acoustics at different levels and are not directly interchangeable.
The challenge is to share their acoustic supervision while preserving each
dataset's original prediction target. This also requires assessing the
benefits and costs for the original tasks and for transfer, and how these
outcomes depend on annotation detail and classification design.

To address this gap, we propose CHORD (Cross-task Harmonization Of Respiratory
Datasets), an annotation-aware framework that learns a shared acoustic
representation and uses attribute predictions to recover each dataset's
native task outputs. Abnormality, crackle,
and wheeze provide common supervision targets that encourage the encoder
to capture the underlying acoustic cues across datasets. ICBHI cycles and
SPRSound events jointly train this encoder and three parallel attribute
predictors; each annotation supervises only the attributes it supports.
Task readouts then map the attribute predictions to the labels required by
each original task. SPRSound uses abnormality alone for
normal/abnormal event classification. For ICBHI, a two-level readout first
predicts whether a cycle is abnormal and, if so, assigns Crackle, Wheeze,
or Both from the attribute scores. We compare single-source and joint
training, fine and coarse labels, and alternative task classifiers to assess
the benefits and costs of shared supervision. HF Lung supports attribute
evaluation and a separate auxiliary-supervision study, while KAUH provides
external evaluation\cite{hsu2021hflung,fraiwan2021kauh}.
