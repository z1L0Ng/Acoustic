\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Automated respiratory sound analysis can support scalable assessment of respiratory conditions by reducing the burden of manually interpreting and annotating auscultation recordings~\cite{hsu2021hflung,bae2023patchmix,kim2024sgscl}.
% However, training these classifiers requires labels that only clinicians can provide, which limits how much data is available~\cite{zhang2024opera}. 
However, developing reliable models still depends heavily on expert annotations that are costly to obtain, limiting the amount and diversity of labeled training data~\cite{zhang2024opera}.
The two main datasets (ICBHI and SPRSound~\cite{rocha2019icbhi,zhang2022sprsound}) that we used in this paper offer an opportunity to expand supervision by reusing labeled recordings. Their annotations describe related acoustic attributes, including crackles, wheezes, and other abnormalities, but differ in granularity and prediction targets, making their task labels not directly interchangeable.

Prior work reuses annotations across datasets by mapping their labels to a shared target. For example, Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2022sprsound} merge crackle categories to combine SPRSound and ICBHI, while LungMix~\cite{ge2025lungmix} maps labels to ICBHI classes (see details in Section~\ref{sec:data_alignment}) for cross-dataset transfer, enabling reuse under an aligned task definition~\cite{hsu2023mixedset}, but coarsening labels can discard distinctions needed by another task. Another line of work represents respiratory sounds within ICBHI through the presence of crackles, wheezes, and their combinations~\cite{zhang2026ocad,chua2024multibreath,jeong2026pcmcl}. Beyond respiratory sound classification, DCASE combines heterogeneous datasets (DESED and MAESTRO Real) for joint sound event detection using class mappings and masked supervision~\cite{cornell2024dcase}. These studies provide ways to align labels and retain their information, but our setting requires sharing supervision across distinct cycle-level and event-level prediction targets. The available annotations can contain finer sound information than a native task requires. Our goal is to provide a framework that shares acoustic supervision across datasets while preserving each one's native prediction target.

The available supervision is therefore richer than the required output. We aim to jointly learn from this information while preserving each dataset's native prediction target.

In this work, we propose LSAA, a joint learning framework that uses shared abnormality, crackle, and wheeze supervision to learn acoustic representations across ICBHI and SPRSound. Each dataset's annotations supervise the acoustic attributes they support. Task-specific readouts map the shared attribute predictions to each dataset's native outputs, allowing fine-grained supervision to guide learning while preserving different native tasks.
We use the training set in HF Lung (HF)~\cite{hsu2021hflung} for optional auxiliary supervision, while reserving testing set in HF and KAUH~\cite{fraiwan2021kauh} for external evaluation. 
Retaining SPRSound's fine-grained labels helps both native tasks even though SPRSound's own task is binary. A LSAA variant with native task heads achieve higher ICBHI Score than explicit attribute readouts with similar crackle/wheeze discrimination on SPRSound. These findings support shared attribute supervision while motivating separate evaluation of task readout designs.
