\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Automated respiratory sound analysis can support scalable assessment of respiratory conditions by reducing the burden of manually interpreting and annotating auscultation recordings~\cite{hsu2021hflung,bae2023patchmix,kim2024sgscl}.
% However, training these classifiers requires labels that only clinicians can provide, which limits how much data is available~\cite{zhang2024opera}. 
However, developing reliable models still depends heavily on expert annotations that are costly to obtain, limiting the amount and diversity of labeled training data~\cite{zhang2024opera}.
Existing public respiratory datasets, such as ICBHI~\cite{rocha2019icbhi}
and SPRSound~\cite{zhang2022sprsound}, offer an opportunity to expand supervision by reusing labeled recordings. Their annotations describe related acoustic attributes, including crackles, wheezes, and other abnormalities, but differ in granularity and prediction targets, making their task labels not directly interchangeable.

Prior work reuses annotations across datasets by mapping their labels to a shared target. For example, Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2022sprsound} merge crackle categories to combine SPRSound and ICBHI, while LungMix~\cite{ge2025lungmix} maps labels to ICBHI classes for cross-dataset transfer. Hsu et al.~\cite{hsu2023mixedset} use mixed-set training on lung and tracheal recordings for breath-phase and continuous adventitious-sound detection. Aligned task definitions enable data reuse, but merging sound categories can discard distinctions needed by another task. Another line of work represents respiratory sounds within ICBHI through the presence of crackles, wheezes, and their combinations~\cite{zhang2026ocad,chua2024multibreath,jeong2026pcmcl}. Beyond respiratory sound classification, DCASE combines heterogeneous datasets (DESED and MAESTRO Real) for joint sound event detection through a class union and masked supervision~\cite{cornell2024dcase}. This motivates sharing fine-grained acoustic supervision across respiratory datasets with different native prediction targets. For example, the available annotations can contain finer sound information than a native task requires but coarsening labels can discard distinctions needed by another task. Our goal is to provide a framework that shares acoustic supervision across datasets while preserving each one's native prediction target.

The available supervision is therefore richer than the required output. We aim to jointly learn from this information while preserving each dataset's native prediction target.

In this work, we propose LSAA, a joint learning framework that combines shared crackle and wheeze supervision with native task losses to learn acoustic representations across ICBHI and SPRSound. Each dataset's annotations supervise the acoustic attributes they support. Dataset-specific native heads predict directly from the shared representation, allowing fine-grained supervision to guide learning while preserving different native tasks.
We use HF Lung training set~\cite{hsu2021hflung} for optional auxiliary supervision (LSAA w/HF), while reserving HF testing set and KAUH~\cite{fraiwan2021kauh} for external evaluation. 
Our experiments support retaining fine-grained supervision: adding shared C/W losses improves mean SPRSound performance despite its binary target, with a small ICBHI decrease. The same supervision also improves mean HF and KAUH performance over native-task training alone. These findings support sharing annotation detail across tasks while assessing native prediction and external transfer separately.
