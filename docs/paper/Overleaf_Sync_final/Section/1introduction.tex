\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\vspace{-3mm}

Automated respiratory sound analysis can support scalable assessment of respiratory conditions by reducing the burden of manually interpreting and annotating auscultation recordings~\cite{hsu2021hflung,bae2023patchmix,kim2024sgscl}.
% However, training these classifiers requires labels that only clinicians can provide, which limits how much data is available~\cite{zhang2024opera}. 
However, developing reliable models still depends heavily on expert annotations that are costly to obtain, limiting the amount and diversity of labeled training data~\cite{zhang2024opera}.
Existing public respiratory datasets, such as ICBHI~\cite{rocha2019icbhi}
and SPRSound~\cite{zhang2022sprsound}, offer an opportunity to expand supervision by reusing labeled recordings. Their annotations describe related acoustic attributes, including crackles, wheezes, and other abnormalities, but differ in granularity and prediction targets, making their task labels not directly interchangeable.

Prior work reuses annotations across datasets by mapping their labels to a shared target. For example, Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2022sprsound} merge crackle categories to combine SPRSound and ICBHI, while LungMix~\cite{ge2025lungmix} maps labels to ICBHI classes for cross-dataset transfer. Hsu et al.~\cite{hsu2023mixedset} use mixed-set training on lung and tracheal recordings for breath-phase and continuous adventitious-sound detection. Aligned task definitions enable data reuse, but merging sound categories can discard distinctions needed by another task. Another line of work represents respiratory sounds within ICBHI through the presence of crackles, wheezes, and their combinations~\cite{zhang2026ocad,chua2024multibreath,jeong2026pcmcl}. Beyond respiratory sound classification, DCASE combines heterogeneous datasets (DESED and MAESTRO Real) for joint sound event detection through a class union and masked supervision~\cite{cornell2024dcase}. None of these lines, however, defines a common set of acoustic attributes shared across datasets for supervision and by native tasks for prediction. For example, the available annotations can contain finer sound information than a native task requires but coarsening labels can discard distinctions needed by another task. Our goal is to provide a framework that shares acoustic supervision across datasets while preserving each one's native prediction target.

The available supervision is therefore richer than the required output. We aim to jointly learn from this information while preserving each dataset's native prediction target.

In this work, we propose LSAA, a joint learning framework that uses shared abnormality, crackle, and wheeze supervision to learn acoustic representations across ICBHI and SPRSound. Each dataset's annotations supervise the acoustic attributes they support. Task-specific readouts map the shared attribute predictions to each dataset's native outputs, allowing fine-grained supervision to guide learning while preserving different native tasks.
We use HF Lung training set~\cite{hsu2021hflung} for optional auxiliary supervision (LSAA w/HF), while reserving HF testing set and KAUH~\cite{fraiwan2021kauh} for external evaluation. 
Our experiments support retaining fine-grained supervision: SPRSound’s detailed labels benefit both native tasks even though its prediction target is binary. The native-head variant LSAA-N w/C+W achieves a higher mean ICBHI Score than the reference LSAA model with explicit attribute readouts, with similar crackle/wheeze discrimination on SPRSound. This readout comparison motivates assessing native-task performance separately from attribute discrimination.
