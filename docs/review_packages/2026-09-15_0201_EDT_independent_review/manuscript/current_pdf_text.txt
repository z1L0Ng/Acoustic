LEARNING SHARED ACOUSTIC A TTRIBUTES ACROSS RESPIRA TORY SOUND
DA TASETS WITH HETEROGENEOUS ANNOTA TIONS
Zilong Zeng, Wade Wu, Arian Azarang, Stephen Xia, Jingping Nie
zilongzeng2030@u.northwestern.edu,wadewu@unc.edu
arian_azarang@med.unc.edu,stephen.xia@northwestern.edu,jingping@unc.edu
ABSTRACT
Respiratory sound datasets provide complementary super-
vision, but differ in annotation granularity and prediction
targets. We propose LSAA (Learning Shared Acoustic At-
tributes), a joint learning framework that shares acoustic
supervision while preserving native tasks. LSAA learns
abnormality, crackle, and wheeze from ICBHI cycles and
SPRSound events, then uses task-specific readouts for four-
class cycle and binary event prediction. This allows fine
sound-type annotations to supervise learning even when the
output is binary. We further assess attribute transfer and aux-
iliary supervision on HF Lung, and external generalization
on KAUH. Across three seeds with official-test-based check-
point selection, LSAA improves ICBHI Score by 3.39 per-
centage points with a 2.13-point SPRSound decrease relative
to the respective single-source models. Further comparisons
support retaining fine annotations under the evaluated train-
ing recipe and reveal task-dependent effects of readout design
and additional supervision.
Index Terms—respiratory sounds, shared supervision,
heterogeneous annotations, acoustic attributes, cross-task
learning
1. INTRODUCTION
Automated recognition of crackles and wheezes can reduce
the manual effort involved in respiratory sound assessment
[1]. Training these classifiers requires expert annotations that
are costly to obtain at scale [2]. Existing respiratory datasets
[3, 4] offer an opportunity to expand supervision by reusing
labeled recordings. Their annotations describe related acous-
tic attributes but differ in granularity and prediction targets,
so their task labels are not directly interchangeable.
To reuse annotations that are not directly interchange-
able, a common strategy is to map source labels to a shared
prediction target, as in SPRSound’s fusion experiments and
LungMix’s cross-dataset transfer [4, 5]. This enables reuse
under an aligned task definition, but coarsening labels can
discard distinctions needed by another task. Representing
sound composition offers a more flexible way to use label
information: Multi-breath and PC-MCL employ multilabel
supervision within ICBHI [6, 7]. Across datasets, DCASE
combines class mappings and masked supervision while re-
taining dataset-specific evaluation within a shared temporal
sound-event detection system [8]. These studies provide
ways to align labels and retain their information, but our set-
ting requires sharing supervision across distinct cycle-level
and event-level prediction targets. For example, SPRSound’s
Wheeze annotations contain information relevant to ICBHI’s
four-class cycle task, although its binary event task requires
only normal/abnormal predictions [4]. The available super-
vision is therefore richer than the required output. We aim
to jointly learn from this information while preserving each
dataset’s native prediction target.
To this end, we propose LSAA (Learning Shared Acous-
tic Attributes). A shared encoder learns acoustic representa-
tions through abnormality, crackle, and wheeze supervision
from ICBHI cycles and SPRSound events. Each annotation
supervises only the attributes it supports. Task-specific read-
outs map the shared attribute predictions to four-class cycle
and binary event outputs, allowing fine supervision to guide
learning while preserving different native tasks.
In this work, we demonstrate:
1. We propose LSAA, a joint learning framework that
connects heterogeneous respiratory sound annotations
through shared acoustic attributes in one model.
2. We develop task-specific readouts over shared abnor-
mality, crackle, and wheeze predictions, enabling fine-
grained supervision to support distinct native outputs.
3. We evaluate native-task performance on ICBHI and
SPRSound, investigate attribute transfer and auxiliary
supervision on HF Lung [1], and assess external gener-
alization on KAUH [9].

2.2. Acoustic Characteristics
To compare acoustic inputs across sources, we prepare mono
16-kHz, 5-s inputs: ICBHI cycles, SPRSound events, and
KAUH recordings are front-truncated or repeated with bound-
ary fading, while HF recordings supply three consecutive
windows. From globally centered 4-kHz analysis copies, we
estimate 80–2000 Hz Welch spectra [10] and compute spec-
tral centroid (power-weighted mean frequency), bandwidth
(frequency spread), and peak frequency. Descriptor medi-
ans are taken within recording and then within patient; HF
uses recording-date groups. Principal component analysis
(PCA) of log-transformed, jointly standardized descriptors
with equal source weights shows source structure with partial
overlap (Figure 1(b)).
To examine whether differences persist within the same
sound categories, we compare ICBHI and SPRSound within
Normal, Crackle, Wheeze, and Both. SPRSound’s Fine/Coarse
Crackle labels are combined as Crackle, and Wheeze+Crackle
corresponds to Both. ICBHI has lower spectral centroids in
every class (Figure 1(c)), showing that input differences
persist after aligning the sound labels. Joint learning must
therefore use compatible acoustic supervision across differ-
ing source distributions.

Providing both readouts does not make single-source
training sufficient for both datasets: each single-source
model performs poorly on the other native task. These
failures accompany the within-label acoustic differences in
Figure 1(b,c). Joint supervision enables one model to serve
both tasks, improving ICBHI Score by 3.39 percentage points
(pp) at a 2.13-pp cost on SPRSound relative to their respec-
tive single-source models. Both Normal recall and correct
abnormal-subclass recall improve on ICBHI. Joint training
therefore expands task coverage, with different gains and
costs relative to the two specialist models.
External benefits depend on the source and prediction
target. LSAA’s HF CAS ranking AUROC (86.44%) is
close to ICBHI-only (87.05%) and exceeds SPRSound-only
(79.25%), while its KAUH patient BA exceeds both controls
(Table 2(a)). Combining sources thus improves KAUH per-
formance without producing a uniform gain across external
targets.
4.2. Supervision and Readout Analysis
A binary prediction target can still benefit from fine super-
vision. Retaining SPRSound C/W labels improves both na-
tive Scores and SPRSound C/W AUROC across three seeds
(Table 2(b)). Coarse SPR’s mean ICBHI Score falls below
ICBHI-only, showing that adding source audio alone does not
ensure a gain under this recipe. The SPRSound improvement
is uneven: Coarse includes an all-Normal seed, while the
other two seeds gain 4.57/0.15 pp. Coarse also removes C/W
calibration and changes relative loss strength, so the compari-
son supports the fine-supervision recipe without isolating an-
notation information alone.

Shared fine supervision does not prescribe a unique task

This supports separate assessment of attribute detection and
native-task performance. LSAA supplies an explicit attribute-
to-task mapping; learned native heads offer another imple-
mentation. Because the alternative retrains both encoder and
heads, the gain cannot be attributed solely to replacing the

More supervision from a source need not improve its eval-
uation target. HF-on lowers CAS AUROC in all three seeds
(−7.41±2.73pp), despite improving SPRSound in two seeds;
KAUH changes direction across seeds (Table 2(c)). HF train-
ing assigns W negatives to eligible Rhonchi/Stridor windows
without Wheeze annotations, whereas CAS evaluation treats
any Wheeze/Rhonchi/Stridor annotation as positive and ranks
recordings using maximum-window Wheeze-head scores. Of
661 CAS-positive test recordings, 256 have no Wheeze anno-
tation. Supervision and evaluation thus target different sound
categories within the same source. This mismatch may con-
tribute to the decline, but the current comparison does not iso-
late its causal effect.
5. CONCLUSION
We presented LSAA, a joint learning framework that con-
nects heterogeneous respiratory sound annotations through

===== PDF PAGE 5 =====
shared acoustic attributes. Task-specific readouts allow one
model to support cycle-level four-class and event-level binary
prediction while retaining fine-grained supervision. Our ex-
periments support retaining fine annotations under the evalu-
ated training recipe and reveal task-dependent tradeoffs from
joint training and readout design. Evaluation on HF Lung and
KAUH extends this assessment beyond the training datasets,
showing that additional supervision does not uniformly im-
prove transfer.
